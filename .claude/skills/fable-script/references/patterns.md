# Fable patterns: what the viral invented-rule stories have in common

**Fable** = an invented world with one impossible rule, told by a kid in the first person. It is the opposite of a
**Fact** script (a real mechanism, told about someone else). Everything here was measured, not guessed:

- **Study set:** the 25 most-viewed of the newest 96 reels on a large animated mini-story account (1.8M to 8.9M views),
  transcribed with word timings, cut-detected and loudness-measured, plus frame sheets of the top 10 (November 2026).
- The source account is deliberately not named, and no line, title or plot from it is reproduced. Every example in this
  kit is original.

If the user has a **style profile** of their own (`style-profiles/fable-*.md`, made by the `study-references` skill),
its numbers override the ones below where they differ.

## The headline

The biggest reels are **2-minute stories built on one strange rule, told by a kid who doesn't fit that rule. The kid
saves someone small and loved by using the rule in a way nobody thought of.** All of the top 5 follow it.

- 24 of 25 state the rule or premise in sentence 1.
- 19 of 25 are long stories (95 to 175 s); 6 of 25 are 36-second gags, which top out lower but are reliable.
- The heart is almost always a younger sibling, a grandparent or a parent. Rarely romance (romance lives in gags).
- The last line echoes the first line or a ritual planted early, with a new meaning.
- The two weakest structures in the set: a comment call-to-action ending and a "part 3" series episode.

## Two formats

| | Fable story | Fable gag |
|---|---|---|
| Share of the study set | 19 of 25 | 6 of 25 |
| Length | 95-175 s (median 130), 414-737 words (median 525) | 36-38 s, 184-195 words |
| Pace | **4.3 words/s** (3.3-4.7) | **5.2 words/s** |
| Opening | the rule in one sentence | a crazy premise in one sentence ("My crush …", "My best friend has ONE rule …") |
| Core | a misfit kid saves a sibling, a grandparent or the town | three escalations of the premise going wrong |
| Ending | callback to the opening or a ritual: warm, or a dread sting | one line of dialogue that flips the premise |

## Two variants of the Fable story

| | Warm (classic 8 beats) | Eerie (crisis-first) |
|---|---|---|
| Use for | funny, warm, bittersweet ideas | eerie, epic, high-stakes ideas |
| Shape | rule → funny proofs → "Me?" → loved one → pressure → turn → sacrifice → callback | rule → **today it broke** (sentence 2-6) → world facts that raise the danger → the loved one who depends on the narrator → turn → a choice that costs → dread sting |
| Tense | usually past | present |
| Share | most of the set | 4 of the top 15, including the #1 |

## The 8 beats (warm Fable story, about 2:00)

| # | Beat | When | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The rule** | 0-5 s | One sentence that breaks reality, plus the price of breaking it. |
| 2 | **Proof montage** | 5-30 s | 3-6 quick funny results of the rule, each with a named person and a number. Comedy and rewatch value live here. Plant one cold fact for later. "You see," may open it (8 of 26 use it), but it is optional. |
| 3 | **"Me?"** | 20-40 s | The narrator doesn't fit the rule. A weakness played as a joke, often a nickname. This trait becomes the solution. |
| 4 | **Loved one + ritual** | 30-60 s | A little sibling, a grandparent, a parent, with a repeated line or gesture that returns at the end. Often an old loss tied to the rule. |
| 5 | **Pressure** | 60-90 s | A selfish authority (mayor, gatekeeper, principal) or a disaster (flood, storm, runaway bus), counted down by weekday or clock time. Adults try and fail. |
| 6 | **The turn** | ~70-75 % in | "That's when I understood." The rule meant something else all along. |
| 7 | **Sacrifice through the rule** | 75-90 % | The narrator uses the misfit trait *through* the rule, and it costs them: years, a voice, a memory, a place. |
| 8 | **Callback** | last 5-10 s | The first line or the ritual returns, changed. Warm stories end on a joke inside the warmth; eerie ones on the threat turning toward the narrator. |

The **Fable gag** keeps only beats 1, 5 and 8: premise, three quick escalations, a one-line dialogue flip.

### Patterns that hold across the set
- **Comedy first, heart second.** The first 20-30 seconds being funny is what buys the emotional turn.
- **Plant and pay off.** A throwaway fact in the montage becomes the twist or the tool. (Example, original: "The
  Hendersons let go of a kite in 1994. They still wave at the sky." → the kite returns in the climax, carrying the
  answer.)
- **Generations mirror.** An old event repeats with the narrator in the elder's place.
- **The darker variant** swaps the rescue for a sacrifice that sticks; the ending is dread, not comfort.
- **Rule of three.** A line repeats three times, raising the stakes each time.

## Hook formulas (sentence 1)

The first sentence is always the premise. Never a cold action with no context, a question to the viewer, or "wait for
it". Examples are original.

| Formula | Example |
|---|---|
| In this world / town, [rule] | "In this town, pennies are worth more than houses." |
| In my town, if you [everyday action], [magic result] | "In my town, if you hum, flowers grow where you stand." |
| We only [action] when [event] | "We only speak when the bell rings. Talk in the silence and you lose your voice for a year." |
| A daily event + what happens to those left behind | "Every midnight the streets swap places. Anyone outside wakes up in someone else's town." |
| A rule that's already broken | "On your tenth birthday, your reflection tells you one secret. Mine stayed quiet." |
| A rule + "except me" | "Everyone in my town has a twin in the lake, except me. Tonight, mine came up." |
| A rule + a teaser of the ending | "…you turn to glass. And the only time I ever turned to glass, I did it on purpose." |
| Eerie: the rule + tonight | "Every sunset, the lighthouse counts us. Tonight it counted one too many." |
| Gag: crush + a literal reading | "My crush said she'd date me when pigs fly, so I bought a pig." |
| Gag: crush + a secret power | "Every time my crush laughs, flowers grow wherever she looks." |

**The strongest hooks add a personal stake in sentence 2 or 3:** a loved one waiting decades, a narrator who is the
exception, a "tonight".

## Pacing and language
- **4.3 words/s** (story), **5.2** (gag). A 2:00 story is about 520 words.
- **Short sentences: median 6 words** (4.5-11), almost none past 20, and about six one-word sentences per story
  ("Nothing." "Eggs." "Gone."). Write for the ear.
- **Specific numbers everywhere:** 312 days, 41 balloons, a 4:12 deadline. Numbers make the impossible feel recorded.
- **Deadpan side jokes inside the danger,** at least three per story. (Original: "Dad tied him to the kitchen table.
  The table floated too.")
- **Kid narrator, 7-12, first person.** Past tense for warm stories, present for eerie ones.
- **Sound words as their own beats:** Clack! Splat. Pfffft. They mark cuts for the animator and get a sound effect.
- **Short dialogue only,** under 10 words a line. The authority figure gets a catchphrase that repeats. (Original:
  the principal's "Rules are rules, Mr. Dale.")
- **No narrating feelings until the reveal.** Show them through action ("He's held it for 312 days.").
- **A name or role for every side character:** the mailman, Mrs. Petrov, the gym teacher.

## Picture: first frame and cut speed

From frame sheets sampled every 0.3-0.5 s.

- **Frame 1 is the strangest image in the story,** already showing the rule. No title card, no slow establishing shot.
- **Detail, then world scale:** one close detail, then pull back within 2 s to show the whole world.
- **One shot per narrated noun,** about a second after it's spoken.
- **Two cut speeds, both work** (median views are the same):
  - *Fast* (bright, comedy, all gags): 5-15 cuts per 10 s, shots of ~1 s, 5-9 cuts in the first 6 s.
  - *Slow* (cinematic, eerie, epic): 1-2.4 cuts per 10 s, shots of 4-8 s, and **no cut in the first 6 s**: one
    continuous move, close on the rule → pull back to the world → push in to the narrator's face.
- **The narrator's reaction ends the hook:** at 3.5-4.5 s, a close-up of the narrator, wide-eyed.
- **Text props carry exposition:** a counter, day cards (MONDAY … THURSDAY), a clock with the deadline, a printed rule
  chart. (Original: a calendar marked "312", a jar labelled "41".)
- **Last shot returns to the first location or prop,** changed.
- **Two looks.** *Bright*: flat sunlit toy-world sets, full frame (warm stories, all gags). *Cinematic*: depth of field,
  dusk and night, often a 3:4 letterboxed picture inside the 9:16 frame (eerie and epic stories).
- **One creature design per story** when the rule has a "someone": a lighthouse that counts, a thing under the stairs.

## Endings
One or two short lines, then cut. No call to action inside the video.

- **Warm callback:** the opening ritual returns with a new meaning.
- **Bittersweet swap:** the narrator now carries what the elder carried.
- **Dread sting:** a calm last line implying it isn't over. (Original: "Tonight, the lighthouse counted me twice.")
- **Mirror line:** the first sentence repeated with one word changed. (Original: "every house holds its breath" →
  "every house breathes out".)
- **Gag flip:** the premise turned back on the narrator in one quoted line.

## Post titles
- **A first-person teaser in caps, ending in "…",** naming the mystery: MY LITTLE BROTHER WON'T LET GO OF HIS BALLOON…
- **The world name in caps:** THE PAPER TOWN, THE TOWN WITHOUT SHADOWS.
- **The rule itself,** in title case: "Every Balloon Takes a Memory".
- One emoji is fine for gags.

## Comment fuel
Top comments argue the rule's logic or solve the mystery before the reveal. Leave **one loophole** viewers will
fight about (original: "Why didn't Dad just pop the balloon?"), and make the twist guessable on a rewatch.

## Sound
Both script types in the study set are mastered loud and steady: about −14 LUFS, a narrow loudness range (~1.5-2 LU).
