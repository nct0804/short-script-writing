# Fact patterns: what the viral true-story shorts have in common

**Fact** = a real mechanism, practice or true event, told as a fast mini-story about someone else. It is the opposite of
a **Fable** script (an invented rule, told by a kid in the first person). Everything here was measured, not guessed:

- **Study set:** 53 shorts from a large animated explainer-story channel: 20 measured in October 2026 and its newest 33
  (0.16M-35.6M views) measured in November 2026 with word timings, cut detection and loudness. Where the two sets
  disagree, the newer one wins.
- The source channel is deliberately not named, and no line, title or plot from it is reproduced. Every example in
  this kit is original.

If the user has a **style profile** of their own (`style-profiles/fact-*.md`, made by the `study-references` skill),
its numbers override the ones below where they differ.

## The shift that matters: from explainer to mini-story
Around mid-October the channel moved from second-person explainers ("If you …") to **third-person mini-stories**, and
the views went up:

| | Explainer (older) | Mini-story (current) |
|---|---|---|
| Length | 31-68 s (median 45) | 55-78 s (median 62) |
| Hook | "If you …", "How do …?" | "He [did the strange thing]." in 15 of 22 |
| Narrator | second person, "you" | an unnamed "he", present tense |
| Ending | the mechanism, sometimes a question to the viewer | a twist or a big visual consequence |
| Median views | 6.8M | 10.1M (and still climbing) |

**Write the mini-story by default** (Fact story). The explainer is still right for pure science with no person in it.

## Three Fact formats
| Format | When | Length · words | Shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fact story** (default) | a real mechanism or practice, acted out by an invented everyman | 55-65 s · 200-245 | deed → setup → So/But/Then → mystery → reveal → payoff |
| **Fact event** | a documented real event with a date and a place | 45-70 s · 170-250 | paradox → setting → escalation → snap → "You see," + analogy → consequence → payoff |
| **Fact explainer** | science with no person in it | 35-50 s · 140-200 | "If you [X], [Y]." → "You see," → 3-5 steps in the second person → mechanism → button |

## Shape: Fact story (55-65 s, ~200-245 words)
| Beat | Job | Length | Measured |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Hook** | One person did something that sounds dumb, mean or impossible. **Past tense, no name.** The deed now, the reason later. | 6-17 words (median 14) | 18 of 33 open "He / They / A man [past-tense deed]." |
| **2. Setup** | One line that makes the world clear: the stakes, the situation. Switch to **present tense**. "You see," optional here. | 1-2 lines | 12 of 33 put "You see," in sentence 2 |
| **3. Escalation** | 4-8 beats chained with **So / But / Then**. Doubters, a boss, a rival or a crowd react. Short **unattributed** quips. 1-2 exaggeration jokes. | most of the video | 27 % of all sentences start with So, But or And |
| **4. Mystery** | The question the viewer is now asking, out loud: "But why?", "Wait, what?", "But how does …?" | 1 short line | in about 2 of 3 |
| **5. Reveal** | **"Well, …"** (after a question) or **"You see, …"**: the one real mechanism, in plain words. Optional everyday analogy. | 1-4 lines | late "You see," in 11 of 33; "question → Well," in 8 of 33 |
| **6. Payoff** | The mechanism *pays off on screen*: the twist lands, or the thing rises, flips, collapses. The video ends on that line's last word. | 1-2 lines | see Endings |

**The reveal can be shown instead of said.** In several of the biggest shorts the mechanism is planted in the
escalation and the climax *shows* it, with no explanation line. Use that when the cause is visible (heat, weight,
pressure, speed); use a spoken reveal when it isn't (psychology, law, a hidden scheme).

## Truth: the mechanism is real, the story can be a parable
Most current shorts are **dramatized parables**: an unnamed "he" in a made-up situation (a cook, a guard, a young
engineer) acts out a mechanism or practice that is real. The script marks the switch to fact with a line like "In real
life, chefs really check eggs this way."

- The **mechanism, practice or event** must be true, with a source in the facts table.
- The **story around it** may be an invented everyman scenario **if it's generic** (no real names, dates or places that
  would make it read as a documented event) and the reveal says what is real.
- Never invent details about a real event, person or place. A **Fact event** is sourced in every detail.
- In the facts table, mark every line **fact** or **dramatized**.

## Pace and language
- **3.4-4.1 words/s** (median 3.7). Pauses are tiny: gaps over 0.25 s add up to ~3 s a minute.
- **Median sentence ~14 words, with a long-short rhythm:** a few 20+-word run-on sentences chained with "and", "so",
  "until", against one-line jabs. (Original: "Raw.")
- **Chain with So / But / Then.** Every sentence is a consequence of the last one. That's where the speed comes from.
- **Present tense** for the story ("He grabs …"); past tense only in the hook.
- **Roles, not names:** "the new cook", "the boss", "his rival". A name only when it's the joke.
- **Unattributed dialogue:** the narrator just says the other character's line, short, with attitude. (Original: "The
  boss storms in. Table nine is reading the menu out loud.")
- **Every line moves:** a new fact, a new image or a bigger stake. If a line re-says the last one, cut it.
- **Never narrate what the viewer sees.** The picture shows the egg spinning; the voice says why it's strange.
- **Enthusiasm from specifics, not adjectives:** numbers, objects, roles.
- **Exaggeration jokes as comparisons.** (Original: "so slow the queue brought snacks".)
- **Tone dial:** *energetic* (brisk, punchy verbs, no exclamation marks) or *deadpan* (one steady tone playing absurd
  facts straight). Pick one and keep it.

## Endings (last 1-2 lines)
| Type | What happens | Share of 33 |
|---|---|---|
| Twist reveal | The setup was a scheme all along | 9 |
| Visual payoff | The mechanism fires on screen (original: the boiled egg rises onto its tip) | 5 |
| Mechanism line | The last line states the rule | 11 |
| Origin | "And that's how [thing] was invented." | 3 |
| Callback joke | A button that turns the hook around | 3 |
| Question to the viewer | older format only; skip it | 2 |

All end on the last word: no outro, no call to action, no "follow for more".

## Picture notes the script plans for
- **Frame 1 is the deed in action** (the hand flicking an egg into a spin).
- **Text props carry facts and jokes:** order tickets, crate stencils, counters, day and date cards, headlines.
  (Original: a crate stencilled "BOILED + RAW" with a sticky note "OOPS"; a ticket "EGG SALAD x40 · 12:00".)
- **An explainer insert for the reveal:** a cross-section, an x-ray, a chart, a chalkboard diagram.
- **The ending is a big visual.** Plan it as the last shot.
- Glides and push-ins rather than hard cuts; crossfades between places; a new composition every 1-2 s from the camera.
- **Captions: one bold white word at a time** (~65 % of the height), keywords in colour.

## Sound
Mastered loud and steady: about −14 LUFS, a narrow loudness range (~2 LU). Music bouncy under the story, a dead stop
right before the reveal word, a button on the last word.
